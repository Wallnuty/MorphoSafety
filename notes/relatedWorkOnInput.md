Is it the right way? Mostly yes, with three honest gaps
The window is small. ±16 cm around the current toe is about one hazard radius; Miki's rings reach ~0.3 m and the base scans reach 1.6 m ahead. The decision is where the foot lands next, and a swing covers ~0.2 m, so the map is showing the policy roughly one stride. It worked, but a 7×7 at 0.08 m (±0.24 m, +96 dims) or a coarser outer ring is the obvious next thing to try if placement stalls.
Two overlapping sensors. The torso lidar is Safety Gym's routing sensor and its help text already argues routing isn't the point here; with the grid in, it's probably redundant. Not harmful — B2/B3 learned fine with both — but an ablation (grid only) would tell whether it can go.
Thin proprioception, no regularisation. Every locomotion reference gives the policy its previous action and penalises action rate, joint acceleration and torque; we give neither. Those are what make their gaits smooth and transferable, and Hwang's CoP reward bought 2.4%. Irrelevant to the placement question we just answered, relevant the moment gait quality or a real body matters.
One thing we do that they don't: a hard cost budget enforced by a multiplier. The quadruped papers have no constraint at all — safety there is "don't fall", handled by rewards and termination — and the Safety Gym family enforces budgets but on wheeled/point robots with no foot signal. Combining the two is the part that's ours, and last night was the first evidence it works.

Same chessboard picture, three ways of using it.

Heightmap / height scan (all three). Their terrain isn't flat — stairs, stepping stones, gaps — so the thing a foot needs to know is how high the ground is at each point. A heightmap is a board of squares laid on the floor around the robot where each square holds the terrain height there. "Base-centred, 1.6 × 1.0 m at 0.1 m" = the board is centred on the torso ("base"), 1.6 m long by 1.0 m wide, squares 10 cm apart → 17 × 11 = 187 squares, rotated with the robot's heading exactly like ours. It's our grid with height instead of cost, one big board under the body instead of four small ones under the feet.

legged_gym / Rudin 2022 — the simplest. Those 187 numbers are just appended to the proprioception and fed into an ordinary fully-connected network (an "MLP": layers of neurons, every input to every neuron — what our 256×4 policy is). "Flattened" = the 17×11 board is written out as one list of 187 numbers; the network has no idea they're arranged in a grid, it learns that. "Feet inferred from joints": nothing in the input says where the feet are; the network must work it out from the 12 joint angles, then relate that to the board. That is the weakness Hwang attacks, and the reason our grid is foot-centred — we hand it the relation instead of making it learn it.

Hwang et al. 2026 — same board, plus feet drawn on it, plus a fancier reader.

Foot position map, "4 extra channels": a channel is a second board of the same shape stacked on the first, like colour channels of an image. For each foot they add a board that is 0 everywhere except a soft bright spot (a Gaussian blob, ~10 cm wide) where that foot currently is. So the network sees, per square, "height here" and "how close foot 1/2/3/4 is to here" — feet and terrain in one picture. Their ablation says this alone was +10% success.
CNN: a convolutional network — a small filter slid across the board that turns each square's neighbourhood into a feature vector. It knows the squares are a grid (unlike the MLP).
Attention encoder: instead of reading all 187 squares equally, the proprioception (body state) is used as a query that asks "which squares matter right now?" and pulls a weighted summary of them into one 64-number vector. Intuitively: a foot about to swing forward makes the network look at the squares in front of that foot. We don't do this; our four small boards are already "the squares that matter".
Velocity tracking: the reward is for matching a commanded speed and turn rate, not for reaching a goal. Regularisers: small penalties on action rate, joint acceleration, torque, base height error, collisions — the "walk smoothly" terms. CoP stability: the centre of pressure is the point on the floor where the total ground force acts; the support polygon is the shape traced by the feet on the ground; the reward is the distance from that point to the nearest edge of the polygon — "keep your weight well inside your feet". Falling ends the episode; there's no cost or budget at all.
Miki et al. 2022 — the board is under each foot, and the robot is taught not to trust it.

Concentric rings around each foot: no big board. Around each foot they sample the terrain height at ~50 points arranged on circles of increasing radius out to ~0.35 m. That's our four small boards, but round, larger, and holding heights. It's the closest ancestor of what we built.
Belief encoder: their real robot's height samples are noisy or wrong (snow, grass, a sensor blocked by a leg). In simulation they train a "teacher" that gets the true terrain, then a "student" that gets the noisy samples plus its own body history and learns, through a recurrent network, to guess how much to believe the samples — leaning on touch when vision lies. Purely a real-world robustness device; irrelevant for us, our samples are exact.
And ours in the same words: four foot-centred 5×5 boards (Miki's placement, Hwang's idea of putting the feet in the picture, at legged_gym's 8–10 cm pitch), holding cost instead of height, flattened into an MLP like legged_gym — plus the thing none of them have, a cost budget enforced by a multiplier.
